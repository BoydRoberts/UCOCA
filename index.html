University of California Online Constitutional Amendment · ucoca.org

Section 1. The People of California find and declare…

  1. 1The people have the right to audit all publicly owned postsecondary courses and programs online at no cost.
  2. 2The people have the right to access — and earn full credit — all publicly owned postsecondary courses and programs online at cost.

Every UC, CSU, and CCC course available online. Free or full credit. Open to all. Fully accredited. Low cost. Drafted. Filed with the California Attorney General twice. Now passing the torch.

Two divisions, one university

The amendment directs the Regents to stand up a separate online university with its own president, faculty, budget, and academic senate — built from courses the public already paid for.

Division I

Free

  • Audit any course, program, or digital library at no cost
  • Question professors, and buy course books and materials at cost
  • Open to anyone, in state or out
  • No credit, no degree

Division II

At cost

  • Full credit and full accredited degrees, fungible with UC
  • Enrollment open to everyone who applies — no gatekeeping
  • Work at your own pace; enroll, test, and finish when ready
  • Tuition includes books, and every line item is published

Who pays for it

Nobody's taxes. The measure funds construction with bonds the university repays out of tuition, and it funds student support out of fees charged to out-of-state students.

It has been done by someone with no experience and no money

Two days after the 2016 election, a 27-year-old in Michigan named Katie Fahey posted on Facebook that she wanted to take on gerrymandering, and asked if anyone would join her. She had no political background. She worked in environmental sustainability.

Michigan required 315,654 valid signatures in 180 days. Her volunteers gathered more than 425,000, from every county in the state, without spending a dollar on signature gathering. They finished ahead of the deadline. Michigan's former state elections director called it nearly unprecedented. The amendment passed with 61 percent of the vote and is now in the Michigan Constitution.

The money came too — about $15.6 million, from roughly 9,000 individual donors. But it arrived after the movement, raised by the people the movement produced. Not before it, from a funder.

California asks for 874,641 valid signatures, gathered within 180 days of the Attorney General issuing a title and summary. It is a bigger number than Michigan's. It is a smaller share of the population than most states require.

If you want to carry it

  1. Read it.The full text is four pages. Start there, not here.
  2. Change whatever you want.The license lets you revise, cut, or rewrite it. It is nine years old and it can be improved.
  3. Get a legal read.An election-law attorney should look at the single-subject rule and the Article IX Section 9 interaction before you file.
  4. File with the Attorney General.Any registered California voter can submit an initiative, with a $2,000 fee refunded if it qualifies. The Attorney General issues a title and summary, and the 180-day clock starts.
  5. Then go get the signatures.This is the part everyone says is impossible without millions of dollars. Michigan proved otherwise in 2018. It is the hardest thing on this list and it is not the wall people tell you it is.

Not in California? Eighteen states let citizens initiate constitutional amendments directly. Several others, including New York, do it through the legislature instead. See below.

It doesn't have to be California

Nothing about this idea is Californian. Every state has public colleges its taxpayers already paid for, and students paying twice for access to them. The text below is a template. Swap the institutions, swap the constitution, keep the argument.

Eighteen states allow citizen-initiated constitutional amendments. Arizona, Arkansas, California, Colorado, Florida, Illinois, Massachusetts, Michigan, Mississippi, Missouri, Montana, Nebraska, Nevada, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, Oregon, and South Dakota. The process is direct in sixteen of them; Massachusetts and Mississippi route through the legislature first, and Mississippi's is currently inoperable.

Compare the percentage, not the number. Requirements are set against different denominators — votes cast in the last election, registered voters, or total population. A big raw number in a big state can be an easier share than a small number in a small one. Work out what fraction of your state's population you actually need before deciding it's out of reach.

Everywhere else, the path is a legislator. States without citizen initiative, New York among them, amend their constitutions through the legislature and then a public vote. That's one sponsor to persuade instead of hundreds of thousands of signatures. A different job, not a closed door.

Ballotpedia maintains current signature requirements and deadlines for every state. Start there.
